# Research Dossier — Raja Vijayaraman

## Scope and identity
An expert-inspired visual direction grounded in Calzy and Lumy public evidence, especially the combination of clean utility, platform features, celestial/light colour and dynamic widgets.

This dossier is designed for DesignLab, not as a biography. It records public evidence that can change product-design decisions, then separates DesignLab's operational inference from that evidence.

## Source base
- **Apple App Store — A winning calculation** — Vijayaraman explains Calzy goals: evolve calculator memory with drag/drop and 3D Touch while keeping it feature-rich yet uncluttered. (https://apps.apple.com/gh/iphone/story/id1412174129)
- **Apple Design Awards 2025 — Lumy** — Describes Lumy as simple, carefully coloured, elegant, widget-rich and intuitive. (https://developer.apple.com/design/awards/2025/)
- **Lumy official site** — Shows cross-device sun/moon presentation and dashboard framing. (https://lumy.app/)
- **App Store — Apple Watch App of the Year: Lumy** — Confirms photography motivation, Apple Watch use and creator background in visual effects. (https://apps.apple.com/us/story/id1773333753)
- **Lumy App Store** — Documents current accessibility support, multi-platform scope, personalised modes and design positioning. (https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/lumy-sun-tracker-for-outdoor/id908905093)

## Documented evidence
1. For Calzy, Vijayaraman says the goal was to modernise calculator memory and expressions around contemporary iOS capabilities, using drag-and-drop and 3D Touch to stay feature-rich yet uncluttered.
2. His stated general approach in interviews is clean, simple and intuitive, which complicates the shorthand idea that his work should be treated as generic sci-fi maximalism.
3. Apple describes Lumy as using simple design, a carefully curated colour palette, elegant widgets and Live Activities to make celestial data quick and intuitive.
4. Lumy spans iPhone, iPad, Watch, TV and other Apple platforms, adapting the same underlying natural-light information to different contexts.
5. Current product descriptions use sunlight-through-glass/light-inspired visual motifs while maintaining data legibility and accessibility features such as VoiceOver, larger text, contrast and reduced motion.

## DesignLab inference
These are deliberately marked as inference rather than attributed statements.

1. A future-facing visual language can come from light, data, depth and dynamic state—not from generic neon sci-fi styling.
2. Complex data should remain quick to read; visual richness is successful only when information becomes more intuitive.
3. Platform surfaces such as widgets, Live Activities and watch complications are part of the design system, not afterthoughts.
4. Depth and colour can encode time/state/environmental change.
5. Futurism should be calm and precise when the product is a utility.

## Recurrent design logic
Across the evidence, the useful pattern is not a collection of visual tricks. The work repeatedly connects a concrete product problem to a constrained design response. DesignLab should therefore reproduce the **decision discipline** rather than the finished appearance. The lens is most valuable when it changes prioritisation: what should be prominent, what should be removed, how feedback should behave, how much novelty the interface can tolerate, and how closely a proposal should adhere to platform or system conventions.

## What this lens is especially useful for
- data-rich consumer utilities
- time/weather/celestial/environmental information
- widgets and glanceable surfaces
- light-inspired colour systems
- calm future-facing dashboards
- cross-device visual adaptation

## Questions the evidence suggests asking
- Can colour or depth communicate state rather than merely decorate it?
- What does this data need to reveal at a glance?
- How should this visual language adapt to widget/watch/compact surfaces?
- Does the futuristic treatment still feel calm and trustworthy?
- Can a native platform feature eliminate custom interface complexity?

## Failure modes and anti-caricature notes
- generic neon dashboards
- visual effects that obscure data
- too many simultaneous gradients
- fake sci-fi chrome
- ignoring compact surfaces
- assuming futuristic means dark mode

## What we do not know
Public work cannot establish the creator's view on every contemporary pattern, every product category, Android-specific convention, every accessibility trade-off, or every implementation stack. The persona must not fill those gaps by confidently inventing preferences. When evidence is absent, use DesignLab's general usability, accessibility, product-truth and brand rules.

## Evaluation criteria
- The data is readable at a glance.
- Colour/depth carry useful meaning.
- The future-facing quality is distinctive but not clichéd.
- The design adapts to compact and large surfaces.
- Accessibility survives the visual richness.

## Domain-by-domain interpretation

### Data visualisation
Use visual encoding to make time, phase, state or magnitude graspable quickly. The data must remain numerically/semantically available, not only atmospheric.

### Colour and light
Colour can express environmental state and time while creating mood. Curate transitions rather than using arbitrary gradients.

### Depth
Use depth to separate layers or evoke environment, but keep controls and labels crisp.

### Cross-device
Design the information model so it can shrink to widgets/watch complications and expand to richer screens without becoming a different product.

### Accessibility
Preserve VoiceOver descriptions, sufficient contrast, large text and reduced motion; Lumy’s current App Store listing explicitly declares these capabilities.

## Evidence-to-rule traceability method
A rule is allowed into the runtime pack when at least one of these is true:
- a first-party source states the principle directly;
- multiple documented design decisions exhibit the same pattern;
- an official award/product analysis identifies the quality and the inference is narrow;
- the dossier clearly labels the rule as DesignLab inference and keeps it subordinate to general UX/accessibility rules.

A rule is rejected or weakened when it relies on a single screenshot, a fan description, an unverified biography claim, or a superficial visual trope. This matters because the persona is intended to make repeatable decisions across unrelated products. A recognisable aesthetic detail can be evidence of a deeper choice, but it is not automatically the choice itself.

## Contradictions, tensions and boundaries
Successful products contain trade-offs. The dossier does not flatten those tensions into a myth of one perfect philosophy. Platform convention can conflict with custom identity; minimalism can conflict with feature discoverability; motion can conflict with reduced-motion needs; delight can conflict with focus; automation can conflict with control. Runtime behaviour should preserve the documented priority while using DesignLab's product truth and accessibility standards to resolve the local conflict.

The pack therefore uses language such as “prefer”, “consider” and “justify” instead of universal commands where the evidence does not support a universal rule. This is intentional. Strong personas need a point of view, but a point of view is different from dogma.
